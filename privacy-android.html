RadScan for Android Privacy Policy

Effective November 22, 2026

RadScan doesn't sell or share your data. There are no accounts, ads, analytics or tracking. The only thing sent to the developer is a scrambled code for your device, when the 5-day evaluation starts (Purchases, below), unless you email support yourself. This page says what the app does with information, in plain words. It matches the Privacy page inside the app (Settings › Privacy).

Your scanner or radio

Local network. RadScan talks directly to your scanner or Icom radio on your own network: its controls, its audio and (a scanner's) FTP server. Nothing passes through any other server. While it listens, RadScan tells other copies of RadScan on your network which scanner it's listening to, under this device's name, so one can take over the audio. That stays on your network.

Scanner FTP logins. Stored on your device, encrypted with a key kept in the Android Keystore, and used only to reach your scanner. FTP isn't encrypted, so use it on your own network.

Icom radio logins. An Icom radio's network user is saved with RadScan's settings, which your device's backup includes. Its password stays on your device only, encrypted with a key kept in the Android Keystore, and is never backed up. They're used only to log in to that radio, which is told a name for your device, like RadScan-1A2B. The radio's login isn't encrypted, so use it on your own network.

Location

RadScan reads your device's location only when you tap Move Scanner Here, or open an Icom radio's map that has airports or weather transmitters to show near you, while the app is open: once, to show you on the map and (Move Scanner Here) to send it once to your scanner. It isn't stored, it isn't sent anywhere else, and it's never read in the background.

Airports and weather transmitters on the map. They come with RadScan, from the FAA's and the National Weather Service's public lists. The map picks the ones near you on your device; nothing is looked up.

The map. The map's pictures come from OpenStreetMap. To draw them, your device asks OpenStreetMap's servers for the area you're looking at; they are kept on your device for a while so they needn't be fetched again.

Ham repeaters on the map. To place repeaters, RadScan looks up their towns with your device's own geocoding service (Google's, on most devices) and, for some, the callsign's public FCC license address at callook.info. Only town names, callsigns from your scanner's favorites or your CHIRP files and a broad search area are sent. Results are kept on your device.

On your device

Recordings are kept on your device only. They leave it only when you share them or save a copy somewhere yourself.

Settings and scanner backups are kept on your device. If Android's backup is on for your device, your settings (saved scanners and radios, scan profiles, alerts) and your scanner backups are included in it, in your own Google account, so a new device or a reinstall gets them back. FTP and Icom radio passwords and recordings are never included.

Alerts are local notifications from your device. Nothing goes through a push server.

Connection log. RadScan notes what happens between it and your scanner (connecting, audio starting and stopping, connection test results), kept on your device. It leaves only inside a support email you send from Help › Contact Support, where you choose what goes with it and can read it first.

Purchases. Buying RadScan is handled by Google through Google Play. RadScan never sees your payment details. So that reinstalling RadScan doesn't start the 5-day evaluation again, the date it began is kept on your device, by Google Play services (with your device's backup, if it's on), and by RadScan's evaluation service. That service gets only that date and a scrambled code made from your device's Android ID, which can't be turned back into it or linked to you. That's why starting the evaluation needs the internet.

Children

RadScan isn't directed at children, and knowingly collects nothing about them.
